This article is an audio-learning recap for anyone who's already read all four articles in the Load Balancing Fundamentals series. Use your browser's or phone's text-to-speech feature and let it play in the background during a commute or while doing chores. There are no diagrams, tables, or code here — just spoken-style prose, stitching the whole series back together into a single, continuous thread.

The series started with what a load balancer actually looks at to decide where to route traffic — the difference between L4 and L7. An L4 load balancer distributes traffic based only on TCP/UDP header information — source and destination IP addresses and port numbers. It never interprets packet content at all, which makes it extremely fast, but it can't do fine-grained control like routing by URL path. An L7 load balancer, by contrast, reads the HTTP request's actual content — URL path, headers, even cookies — before deciding where to send it. That flexibility comes at a higher processing cost. And all a client ever directly knows is a single virtual IP address, the VIP, published by the load balancer — which backend server is actually doing the work stays completely hidden.

Next came the routing algorithm and the health check mechanism. Round robin is the simplest approach, but when requests vary in how long they take to process, it ends up skewing load toward one specific server. The least-connections algorithm instead makes its decision dynamically, based on the real load of connections currently being processed, which handles that skew. We also covered session persistence — sticky sessions — pinning a given client to the same server every time. That's a mechanism you need when the backend holds session information in its own memory, but it's a trade-off that sacrifices some of load balancing's own distribution effectiveness. On health checks, we covered the active and passive approaches, and the fall/rise threshold concept for preventing flapping. What mattered most here was keeping two layers separate in your head: a health check decides the pool of routing candidates, while an algorithm decides which server within that pool actually gets picked.

Then came SSL termination and SSL passthrough — the design decision of where a TLS certificate actually lives. SSL termination decrypts TLS at the load balancer and relays plaintext HTTP to the backend, with the advantage of consolidating certificate management into one place. SSL passthrough never decrypts anything at the load balancer, relaying traffic still encrypted — keeping encryption end-to-end, but losing access to L7 features. We also covered an important exception: even in SSL passthrough, looking at just the SNI information included in plaintext in the TLS handshake's first message still lets you route by hostname. We touched on a third approach too, SSL bridging, splitting the difference between the other two. Which approach to pick comes down to weighing two axes against each other: certificate management overhead, and how strict your encryption requirement actually is.

The final article put everything from the lecture into practice, hands-on, actually building it with HAProxy. You watched requests alternate between two backend servers under round robin, confirmed by looking at the actual response content. Then you deliberately stopped one backend server, and confirmed the client never saw a single error — every request simply got routed to the healthy backend instead. That was proof the health check mechanism from the lecture was actually doing its job. Behind the seemingly unremarkable outcome of "the service keeps running even through a failure," you got to confirm, with your own hands, that a concrete mechanism was genuinely at work.

Looking back across all four articles, one consistent pattern emerges. The difference in what information L4 and L7 actually look at, the two separate layers of algorithm and health check, the certificate-placement trade-off between SSL termination and passthrough — every one of them shared the same shape: behind what looks like one single action, "routing traffic," several independent decision axes are actually combined together. Looking at a load balancer's configuration in front of you, and asking "is this L4 or L7," "is this an algorithm problem or a health check problem," "where should the certificate actually live" — pulling those axes apart and asking again. That's the perspective a top-1% engineer carries away from this series. And that's the recap of the Load Balancing Fundamentals series, complete.
